# Eight pillar playbooks

The eight pillars organize research. Every pillar report must contain a direct answer, a contrary case, an evidence table, and an experiment. The checklist is **not** a license to assert unavailable facts.

## P1 Problem quality (20% weight)
- Who feels the pain? How often, how recently, and with what quantified losses (time, money, reliability, legal exposure, emotional costs)?
- What do customers use today: spreadsheet, labor, competing tool, delegation, no action?
- Triggering event and urgency, and who has budget or authority to solve it?
- Countercase: the pain is tolerated, rare, legislated away, or only salient to enthusiastic early adopters.
- Key test: 10–15 diverse, consented qualitative problem interviews **proposed**, not pretended; or documented real behavior. Define recruiting and segment scope.

## P2 Market/customer (15%)
- Precisely separate economic payer, user, influencer, gatekeeper; initial ICP with firmographics/psychographics, willingness and budget constraints.
- Bottom-up TAM/SAM/SOM by eligible account or transaction; cite counts, price units, date, geography. Compare top-down sanity bounds without conflating them.
- Check budget cycle, buyer reachability, concentration, reachable wedge and adjacent segment expansion.
- Countercase: vast reported industry yet tiny, inaccessible or fragmented payable niche.
- Key test: sourced ICP account list and qualified buyer discovery; demand commitments later.

## P3 Value proposition (15%)
- Current job-to-be-done, core outcome, time-to-value, measurable lift, switching/migration cost, feature vs complete workflow.
- Compare against best available alternative and status quo on *customer-important* criteria, not feature count.
- What advantage survives a competitor shipping a clone? Clearly differentiate the initial wedge from durable defensibility (also P6).
- Countercase: nice-to-have, feature inside incumbent, no need to change workflow.
- Key test: interactive concept/concierge pilot where success is observed completed task, saved time or explicit paid commitment.

## P4 Business economics (15%)
- Specify buyer and charged unit, pricing hypotheses, competitors' public prices with dates and tiers, willingness-to-pay gaps.
- Low/base/high modeled revenues, direct fulfillment cost, payment/compute/support/sales burden, gross margin, contribution, break-even, working capital and time-to-cash.
- Clearly separate CAC *unknown* from testable CAC budget; churn and renewal are unknown without cohorts.
- Countercase: high gross revenue but negative contribution, cash-intensive customer onboarding, adverse unit economics at small scale.
- Key test: real priced offer to budget-holders and bounded delivery pilot measured costs.

## P5 Distribution (15%)
- First 10 plausible paying customers and why this channel reaches them, buyer journey, incentives, gatekeepers, procurement, sales cycle, distribution costs.
- Compare founder-led sales, outbound, inbound, partnerships, community, app marketplaces; identify channel competition and constraints.
- Initial outreach and expected conversion are hypotheses; do not invent funnel benchmarks.
- Countercase: expensive-to-reach customers, reliance on platform algorithm, reseller bottlenecks, multi-month sales cycle.
- Key test: small permission-based outreach/sales experiment with explicit conversion and cost metrics.

## P6 Competition & moat (10%)
- List named direct competitors, indirect substitutes, open-source, current enterprise/DIY solutions and 'do nothing'.
- Verify pricing/features/differentiators with primary URLs. Explain target segments and distribution power, not just logos.
- Identify copyability, switching costs, network effects, proprietary data rights, contracts, regulation, brand/trust, acquisition advantage; distinguish proposed from existing.
- Countercase: easy incumbent bundling, network effects in competitor's favor, no structural advantage.
- Key test: buyer win/loss interviews, blind workflow comparison with incumbent.

## P7 Execution & founder fit (5%)
- Minimum deliverable, critical-path dependencies, integration, accuracy, operations, team gaps, security/privacy, legal, data licensing, cost and lead time.
- Founder-market fit only from actual founder experience supplied; otherwise label unknown.
- Define the **smallest safe paid/concierge pilot**, not a six-month build. Distinguish proof of concept from ongoing reliable product delivery.
- Countercase: licenses unavailable, dependency pricing prohibitive, operational cost too high, data access blocked.
- Key test: short technical proof, compliance specialist review, partner validation, service prototype.

## P8 Timing & survival (5%)
- Why the gap is actionable now: a dated technology cost change, regulation, demographic/behavior change, channel opening, incumbent weakness or new capability.
- Consider substitute technical solutions emerging, platform API policy changes, macro budgets, cyclicality, renewal timing, and possible anti-tailwinds.
- Adjacent expansion should not rescue a failed initial market thesis.
- Countercase: market too early/late, blocked incentives, supply chain/legislative uncertainty, platform captures value.
- Key test: monitor a concrete leading indicator, customer budget timing, supplier commitment or regulation milestone.

## Crosscutting suitability gates

Regulated obligations, safety, illegal acquisition methods, material privacy/security risk, impossible access to critical proprietary data, fundamental patent/IP obstacles, and unsustainable finances may override weighted scores. The analysis **identifies and sources** risks but does not certify legal compliance.

## Stage discipline

Idea/desk-research stage: score opportunity hypotheses but mark unvalidated product demand. Discovery stage: demonstrated problems and buyer workflow. Pilot stage: real stakeholder actions, willingness to pay, unit costs and early retention. Scaling stage: multi-cohort measured economics and repeatable channel, outside the scope of desk-only research.
